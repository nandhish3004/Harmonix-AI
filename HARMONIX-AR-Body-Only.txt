Printed circuit boards and thin metal covers shake when nearby fans or motors run. When that shaking hits a natural pitch of the board, bending strain climbs and solder joints can crack. Most engineers check this on a laser Doppler bench or a physical shaker table, both of which require costly bench gear and manual setup. We built HARMONIX-AR to run this test in a phone web browser backed by a Python server. The phone camera tracks the four board corners using color-Sobel edges and skin masking, while DINOv2 on the server clears out cluttered desk backgrounds. Tapping the board once gives a short sound clip. A zero-padded fast Fourier transform with parabolic curve fitting pulls out the pitch, and an Audio Spectrogram Transformer checks the tap sound. Those board dimensions and pitches feed a Kirchhoff-Love plate solver. With Gaussian weights at each screw point, it maps nodal sand lines and bending stress onto the camera view, then estimates Basquin fatigue life. Monte Carlo benchmarks across four common board materials confirm sub-hertz pitch accuracy and sub-millisecond solver latency.

Flat boards and thin sheets hold parts together in consumer electronics and aircraft panels. Printed circuit boards and metal covers shake whenever motors or fans run nearby. Every board has natural speeds where it shakes hard, called eigenmodes. At those speeds, some lines stay still while nearby spots bend up and down. Solder joints sitting on those bending spots wear out and crack.

Testing a board on a desk with only a phone is tricky. Desk clutter and fingers sit in the camera view, so plain edge finders grab the wrong box. A finger tap also rings out fast. Standard fast Fourier transforms smear that short ring across wide bins and miss the true pitch. Screws and standoffs make it harder still because they hold the board still at pins and shift where stress lands.

Lab tools solve these steps separately, at high cost. Laser Doppler systems read tiny shakes accurately, yet stay locked in metrology rooms. Shaker tables with sand show still lines directly, though sand dirties live boards and needs rigid clamps. Finite-element software computes stress well, while forcing the user to build a new mesh for every board. Learned vision, audio, and physics-informed models advance individual stages, yet no browser tool combines live board tracking with tap spectroscopy and pinned plate mechanics.

We built HARMONIX-AR to run inside a normal web browser with a small PyTorch server behind it. The phone handles corner tracking and mic capture. The server runs DINOv2 for clean masks and an Audio Spectrogram Transformer for tap checks. Kirchhoff-Love math then turns the board size and tap pitch into live augmented reality sand lines and stress maps.

To keep the phone responsive, the browser shrinks each camera frame to 144 pixels wide and blurs it with a 5-tap filter. Pixels matching human skin colors in YCbCr space get masked out before edge math runs, so fingers cannot steal the box. Color-Sobel gradients then pull out strong edges, and a 4-connected region pass fits the tightest tilted rectangle around the board. If PCB traces break up the edges, a 48-bin Otsu threshold over a center-weighted color score recovers the solid board shape.

When the desk is too messy, the browser sends the frame to our PyTorch server running DINOv2. We run DINOv2 on the normal image and on a flipped copy, then average both attention maps. An Otsu threshold turns that map binary, and largest-connected-component filtering keeps only the biggest blob so stray desk objects drop out before finding the corners.

Before tapping, the mic listens to the room briefly to measure noise and compares low versus mid energy to adjust for phone mic roll-off. A clip counts as a tap only if its RMS loudness beats room noise by 2.6x and its crest factor reaches at least 3.4. Speech and desk bumps get ignored. Each clean tap clip is multiplied by a Hann window and zero-padded to 16,384 points before taking the FFT. Around the tallest bin, a three-point log-parabolic fit finds the refined pitch.

We test HARMONIX-AR on four common plates: FR-4 PCB, Aluminum 6061-T6, Carbon Steel, and Cast Acrylic. Sound tests run 20 Monte Carlo trials per mode on damped sine waves with 20 dB white noise at both 16 kHz and 44.1 kHz across four FR-4 pitches. Vision post-processing tests Otsu-LCC on 30 noisy attention maps with an added clutter block. Metal plates shake faster than plastic or FR-4 because their stiffness-to-weight ratio is higher. Zero-padding to 16,384 points and fitting the log parabola cuts pitch error to sub-hertz levels across every tested mode, taking under a millisecond per tap. Adding LCC filtering to Otsu thresholding drops the stray clutter island and lifts mean IoU to full overlap in a few milliseconds.
